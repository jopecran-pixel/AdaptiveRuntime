Authority for AI agents

Access is not authority.

Trust in an agent does not become unlimited authority.

AI agents already pay invoices, change records and prepare decisions, with the credentials they were given. Adaptive Runtime sits between agents and the tools they use. Before each action, it decides whether the action goes ahead, waits for a person or is refused, and keeps evidence of that.

The problem

An agent with access to a system can do everything that access allows.

The agent holds the keys of whoever set it up.

Credentials open the whole system. They do not say up to what amount, to whom, or on whose behalf.

A document can give it orders.

An invoice, an email or a ticket can carry hidden instructions. The agent reads them as if they came from its user.

In the end, no one knows who authorised it.

Logs show what ran. They do not show who authorised it, under which rules, or whether what ran is what was approved.

What it does

The organisation's systems do not change. Authority moves to a place where it can be seen, decided and proven.

Identifies

Which agent is asking, and on behalf of which person, verified with the organisation's identity provider.

Decides

By the organisation's rules and limits, and by what its systems know at that moment.

Calls a person

When the action commits the organisation. The approver sees what will happen, and the requester does not approve.

Proves

Each decision goes into a sealed record. For each approved action, it checks that what ran is what was approved.

Evidence for auditors and supervisors

Each decision is recorded: who asked, on whose behalf, who approved and under which rules. That record helps demonstrate the human oversight required by the AI Act (Art. 14), and the control and record-keeping required by DORA, the GDPR and NIS2 (Art. 21).
